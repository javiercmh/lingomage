# Reading, glossary support, and repeated encounters

- **Question:** What do glossed reading, incidental learning, repeated encounters and extensive reading imply for generated stories with English notes?
- **Reviewed:** 2026-10-09; revised 2026-10-19 (E1 and E3 abstracts re-checked, metadata via Crossref, E4 added); 2026-11-26 (E5 added). Scoped review via Consensus; publication metadata and available original text checked separately. Access is stated per source.
- **Informs:** [PED-002](../pedagogy/PED-002-contextual-reading-support.md), [PED-004](../pedagogy/PED-004-level-guided-generation.md). Related: [retrieval and spacing](spaced-review.md), [instruction, feedback and production](instruction-feedback-listening-production.md).

Keys: E1 `webbEtAl2023IncidentalMetaAnalysis`, E2 `yanagisawaWebbUchihara2020Glossing`, E3 `uchiharaEtAl2019Repetition`, E4 `sangersEtAl2025ExtensiveReading`, `nakanishi2015ExtensiveReading` and `hamada2020ExtensiveReadingBias`.

## E1 — Meaning-focused vocabulary learning

Webb, S., Uchihara, T., & Yanagisawa, A. (2023). *How effective is second language
incidental vocabulary learning? A meta-analysis*. Language Teaching, 56, 161–180.
[DOI: 10.1017/S0261444822000507](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0261444822000507).

Access: original full-text methods and results inspected. Synthesis of 24 studies,
29 effect sizes, and 2,771 participants, covering reading, listening, combined
input, and viewing. Learners and proficiency levels varied.

Meaning-focused input supported vocabulary gains, but learning rates were partial
and heterogeneous. Reading yielded mean target-word learning rates of 17% on
immediate and 15% on delayed tests. Different assessments measure different
knowledge; those percentages are not predictions for an individual learner.

Decision: offer short stories as reading practice, not as guaranteed mastery.
Learner-oriented text and simple language are reasonable starting constraints.
AI generation, four specific target languages, and the selected story length were
not evaluated as LingoMage's intervention; transfer remains an assumption.

## E2 — Glossing

Yanagisawa, A., Webb, S., & Uchihara, T. (2020). *How do different forms of glossing
contribute to L2 vocabulary learning from reading?* Studies in Second Language
Acquisition, 42, 411–438.
[DOI: 10.1017/S0272263119000688](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0272263119000688).

Access: original publisher abstract inspected; full methods not reviewed.
Meta-analysis of 42 studies, 359 effect sizes, and 3,802 participants comparing
gloss formats and glossed versus nonglossed reading.

Glossed reading supported greater learning; L1 glosses outperformed L2 glosses in
the synthesis. However, glossaries were among the weaker formats, and learning
varied with assessment, comprehension, and proficiency.

Decision: retain the requested English contextual glossary for English-speaking
learners. Do not call it the most effective format. Glossary placement and future
interactive formats remain design questions. These studies do not validate AI
translations or guarantee that English is every user's first language.

## E3 — Repeated encounters

Uchihara, T., Webb, S., & Yanagisawa, A. (2019). *The effects of repetition on
incidental vocabulary learning: A meta-analysis of correlational studies*.
Language Learning, 69, 559–599.
[DOI: 10.1111/lang.12343](https://doi.org/10.1111/lang.12343).

Access: original publisher abstract inspected. Synthesis of 26 studies,
45 effect sizes, and 1,918 participants examining encounters and learning.
The reported association was moderate (r = .34), with learner, treatment, and
measurement moderators.

Decision: request natural vocabulary reuse and allow another story from the same
words. This correlational synthesis does not establish a required repetition
count or a causal effect of LingoMage's regeneration feature. Avoid forcing a
fixed encounter count that makes stories unnatural.

## E4 — Extensive reading

Sangers, N. L., et al. (2025). *Learning a language through reading: A meta-analysis
of studies on the effects of extensive reading on second and foreign language
learning*. Educational Psychology Review, 37(4), 96.
[DOI: 10.1007/s10648-025-10068-6](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-025-10068-6).

Access: Crossref metadata and abstract summary only. The Springer page redirected to
a sign-in handshake, so full text was not read (the record states CC BY 4.0).
Positive effects, small to medium, across reading comprehension, vocabulary,
decoding/fluency, motivation, writing, oral proficiency and general proficiency.
Effects were larger when learners' text choice was limited and when some form of
accountability was included, and larger when samples had mixed first languages.
Nakanishi, T. (2015). *A meta-analysis of extensive reading research*. TESOL
Quarterly, 49(1), 6–37. [DOI: 10.1002/tesq.157](https://doi.org/10.1002/tesq.157).
Access: Crossref metadata only; the 34 studies/d = 0.46 (group contrasts) and d = 0.71
(pre-post) figures come from the Consensus abstract, so they are provisional.
Hamada, A. (2020). *Using meta-analysis and propensity score methods to assess
treatment effects toward evidence-based practice in extensive reading*. Frontiers in
Psychology, 11, 617. [DOI: 10.3389/fpsyg.2020.00617](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2020.00617).
Access: open-access full text, read through a summarizing fetch tool (first 100,000
of about 128,800 characters). A meta-analysis of 22 studies gave d = 0.55, reduced to
d = 0.37 when restricted to studies with equivalent groups (selection bias); a
propensity-score study of 109 reading learners versus 115 controls (one Japanese
university, one semester) gave d = 0.24–0.44. Stated limits: non-randomized, single
institution, unobserved confounders, no standard definition of reading amount.

Decision: reading-first practice is supported, with small-to-medium and possibly
overestimated effects. The evidence concerns sustained reading of many texts over a
term, mostly with English-target classroom learners; it does not test one-off AI-written
chapters, the four target languages or reading amounts a learner would choose.
Because effects were larger with limited text choice and accountability, learner-chosen
briefs and unlimited generation are not shown to be optimal. The related suggestions
(accountability, a gradual challenge increase) have not been adopted; they are
[future directions](../product/future-directions.md) questions.

## E5 — Lexical coverage and gloss density

Reviewed 2026-11-26 via Consensus search records (abstracts only unless stated); informs the Vocabulary notes setting in PED-002.

- **Coverage and comprehension.** Laufer & Ravenhorst-Kalovski (2010, `lauferRavenhorstKalovski2010LexicalThreshold`) propose about 95% known words as a minimal and 98% as an optimal threshold for reading. Schmitt, Jiang & Grabe (2011, `schmittJiangGrabe2011PercentageKnown`, n = 661) found a roughly linear relation without a sharp threshold. Webb, Pellicer-Sánchez & Wang (2025, `webbEtAl2025CoverageRevisited`, partial replication of Hu & Nation 2000, advanced learners) found only 100% coverage significantly better than 90% and 95%. Herman & Leeser (2022, `hermanLeeser2022LexicalCoverage`) found 98% predicted recall of 8 of 10 main events for second-semester Spanish learners reading graded texts.
- **Gloss density.** Taylor (2014, `taylor2014GlossingFrequency`, meta-analysis) found the largest L2 reading-comprehension effects in computer-based studies glossing 50% or more of target items and the second largest with 5% or less, so no monotonic density rule follows. Majuddin (2014, `majuddin2014GlossDensity`, n = 33) found no comprehension trade-off between high and low density but less uptake of unglossed words. A review (Lofgren 2022) reports a study whose authors suspected they glossed too many words, without a number.

Decision: use coverage as the rationale for the Standard target ("about 49 of every 50 running words" understood with notes) and make density a learner setting because no study fixes an optimum. Limits: coverage studies count known words, mostly in English and with university learners; they do not test AI notes, tap-to-open glosses or these four languages, and they do not show that a note is equivalent to a known word.

## Product assumptions and remaining review

Glossary placement, local rereading, the optional planned focuses and narrative scope ([PED-004](../pedagogy/PED-004-level-guided-generation.md)) are product choices; the evidence above does not validate those bounds or the planning/writing split. (An earlier version of this review described a retired design with five to ten target words, fixed story lengths and A1-oriented suggestions.) No learning trial has been run. Language-specific grammar progression and beginner readability need deeper review; content checks must not be reported as retention evidence.
