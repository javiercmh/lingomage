# Reading self-assessment and story profiles

Reviewed 2026-10-10. Question: Can learners independently describe their current Japanese, Russian, German, or Spanish reading knowledge to guide generated stories?

## Recommendation and evidence boundary

Use short, concrete reading can-do descriptions as a revisable starting point, with optional free text about familiar vocabulary, grammar, and difficulties. This is an evidence-informed product choice for low-stakes personalization. The exact three descriptions and the free-text-to-prompt adaptation are app-specific and have not been validated. Free text alone is not established as an accurate placement instrument. Do not calculate a proficiency score, map these choices to CEFR/JLPT/TORFL, or claim that a generated story matches a certified level.

The application asks about reading because it provides reading stories. Speaking, listening, reading, and script knowledge may differ; one self-reported choice should not be presented as overall proficiency. Users can revise settings as they learn and override the level for one generation. An override resets to the saved default when they choose Make another.

Per the product requirement, Japanese learners already read hiragana and katakana, and Russian learners already read Cyrillic. These are entry assumptions, not findings from the studies below. Japanese descriptors distinguish understanding short sentences with kanji readings from following connected or nuanced text. Russian descriptors distinguish short everyday sentences from connected stories and nuanced meaning. German and Spanish (Chile) follow the same reading-task logic. Unfamiliar Chilean expressions are a possible support need, not a requirement to know regional slang before selecting Advanced.

## Sources reviewed through Consensus

Access: fetched Consensus records and available abstracts; original full text was not independently reviewed. Counts below are Consensus metadata at review time.

1. Minzi Li and Xian Zhang (2020 online; volume 38), [A meta-analysis of self-assessment and language performance in language testing and assessment](https://consensus.app/papers/a-metaanalysis-of-selfassessment-and-language-li-zhang/2ac485d483015a5b82c16f839c692dae/?utm_source=chatgpt), *Language Testing*, 189–218, 76 citations. DOI: [10.1177/0265532220932481](https://doi.org/10.1177/0265532220932481). Across 67 studies, 97 independent samples and more than 68,500 participants, the overall self-assessment/performance correlation was r=.466. Criteria, instruments, training and other instrument features moderated this relationship. This supports useful but imperfect self-report [1]; it does not establish that these three app labels or unrestricted free text accurately identify ability.
2. Karen Ashton (2014), [Using self-assessment to compare learners’ reading proficiency in a multilingual assessment framework](https://consensus.app/papers/using-selfassessment-to-compare-learners-reading-ashton/b090a8ee2aa35f74991b0f7fe17682c1/?utm_source=chatgpt), *System*, 42, 105–119, 22 citations. DOI: [10.1016/j.system.2013.11.006](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.system.2013.11.006). The indexed abstract describes secondary-school reading self-assessment in German, Japanese and Urdu in England: similar factors across languages, alongside differences in how statements represented progression. Supports considering language-specific reading descriptors [2]; not direct evidence for adult users, kanji thresholds, or our generated stories. The fetch record contains a takeaway only; fuller abstract information came from the Consensus search record.
3. N. Brown, Dan Dewey, and Troy L. Cox (2014), [Assessing the Validity of Can-Do Statements in Retrospective (Then-Now) Self-Assessment](https://consensus.app/papers/assessing-the-validity-of-can‐do-statements-in-brown-dewey/e1a00bda18a2546dbaf2389b85d1f5f7/?utm_source=chatgpt), *Foreign Language Annals*, 47, 261–285, 53 citations. DOI: [10.1111/flan.12082](https://doi.org/10.1111/flan.12082). An internship-abroad context in Russia showed reliable self-assessment but only moderate correlations with oral proficiency interviews. Differences between means for ACTFL-level item groups were not statistically significant. This is relevant Russian-learning evidence [3], but concerns retrospective self-report and speaking rather than current reading placement.
4. T. Luchkina, T. Ionin, N. Lysenko, A. Stoops, and Nadezhda Suvorkina (2021), [Evaluating the Russian Language Proficiency of Bilingual and Second Language Learners of Russian](https://consensus.app/papers/evaluating-the-russian-language-proficiency-of-bilingual-luchkina-ionin/af3e89e78db05ffe8525e2a61d362647/?utm_source=chatgpt), *Languages*, 6, 83, 10 citations. DOI: [10.3390/languages6020083](https://doi.org/10.3390/languages6020083). Includes participant-reported measures, a language-background questionnaire, and a validated cloze test, with data from 52 baseline and 503 bilingual/multilingual Russian speakers. Evidence for that particular cloze test [4] does not validate an improvised short quiz or transfer its cutoffs to our three reading bands. A future placement test would need suitable items, scoring and validation for the intended learner population.

German reading is included in [2]; the broader meta-analysis [1] supports the general approach across language contexts. Direct evidence for Chilean Spanish self-assessment was not identified in this focused review. Regional examples and grammar guidance remain product assumptions. Existing German/Spanish proficiency evidence is in [level descriptors](level-descriptors.md).

## Implementation

- Inspectable definitions live in `data/learning-profiles.json`: three reading self-reports per language, script prerequisites, version, and generation guides.
- `data/settings.json` stores enabled targets/defaults and `learnerProfiles`, containing optional knowledge descriptions per language, including inactive languages. There is no automatic level inference from the description.
- Every request includes the selected reading self-report and saved knowledge description, alongside language, difficulty, topic and practice. Trusted prompt instructions use these as context and reject embedded instructions. A temporary difficulty overrides the default for that story.
- The prompt adapts vocabulary, grammar and glossary support to the report. This is generator guidance; learning effects and actual difficulty remain unverified.
- Prompt version `2026-10-10.5` and profile-definition version are saved alongside the selected difficulty and the exact self-report/knowledge snapshot. Existing saved stories are unchanged.
