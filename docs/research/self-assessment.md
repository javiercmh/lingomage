# Reading self-assessment and story profiles

Reviewed 2026-10-10. Question: Can learners independently describe their current Japanese, Russian, German, or Spanish reading knowledge to guide generated stories?

Decision: [PED-001](../pedagogy/PED-001-learner-self-report.md). Bibliographic metadata: [references.bib](references.bib). Source keys: [1] `liZhang2021SelfAssessment`, [2] `ashton2014ReadingSelfAssessment`, [3] `brownDeweyCox2014CanDoValidity`, [4] `luchkinaEtAl2021RussianProficiency`; follow-up sources `suzuki2015JapaneseSelfAssessment` and `tigchelaarEtAl2017CanDoValidity`.

## Recommendation and evidence boundary

Use short, concrete reading can-do descriptions as a revisable starting point, with optional free text about familiar vocabulary, grammar, and difficulties. This is an evidence-informed product choice for low-stakes personalization. The exact three descriptions and the free-text-to-prompt adaptation are app-specific and have not been validated. Free text alone is not established as an accurate placement instrument. Do not calculate a proficiency score, map these choices to CEFR/JLPT/TORFL, or claim that a generated story matches a certified level.

The application asks about reading because it provides reading stories. Speaking, listening, reading, and script knowledge may differ; one self-reported choice should not be presented as overall proficiency. Users can revise settings as they learn and override the level for one generation. An override resets to the saved default when they choose Make another.

Per the product requirement, Japanese learners already read hiragana and katakana, and Russian learners already read Cyrillic. These are entry assumptions, not findings from the studies below. Japanese descriptors distinguish understanding short sentences with kanji readings from following connected or nuanced text. Russian descriptors distinguish short everyday sentences from connected stories and nuanced meaning. German and Spanish follow the same reading-task logic. Learners can specify a Spanish regional variety separately; regional expressions are not assumed in the self-assessment.

Criteria, instruments, training and other instrument features moderated this relationship. This supports useful but imperfect self-report [1]; it does not establish that these three app labels or unrestricted free text accurately identify ability.
2. Karen Ashton (2014), [Using self-assessment to compare learners’ reading proficiency in a multilingual assessment framework](https://consensus.app/papers/using-selfassessment-to-compare-learners-reading-ashton/b090a8ee2aa35f74991b0f7fe17682c1/?utm_source=chatgpt), *System*, 42, 105–119, 22 citations. The indexed abstract describes secondary-school reading self-assessment in German, Japanese and Urdu in England: similar factors across languages, alongside differences in how statements represented progression. Supports considering language-specific reading descriptors [2]; not direct evidence for adult users, kanji thresholds, or our generated stories. Differences between means for ACTFL-level item groups were not statistically significant. This is relevant Russian-learning evidence [3], but concerns retrospective self-report and speaking rather than current reading placement.
4. T. Luchkina, T. Ionin, N. Lysenko, A. Direct evidence for Spanish self-assessment was not identified in this focused review. Regional examples and grammar guidance remain product assumptions. ## Follow-up review: knowledge-field examples

Rechecked Consensus on 2026-10-10 and fetched the records below. The examples are entirely in English and describe familiar communicative reading tasks and support needs, rather than listing grammar forms or repeating the reading bands. They assume kana/Cyrillic literacy. The helper asks learners to describe something they have actually understood; examples are illustrative, not requirements or validated assessment items.

- Yuichi Suzuki (2015), [Self-assessment of Japanese as a second language: The role of experiences in the naturalistic acquisition](https://consensus.app/papers/selfassessment-of-japanese-as-a-second-language-the-role-of-suzuki/51b112ffa1415a8a8a2f058e53f5dbab/?utm_source=chatgpt), *Language Testing*, 32, 63–81. DOI: [10.1177/0265532214541885](https://doi.org/10.1177/0265532214541885). Consensus citation count: 38. Experience affected self-assessment errors; tasks with which participants had little experience showed greater misalignment. This motivates asking about actual experience, without validating our reading examples.

## Implementation

- The optional knowledge-field examples describe specific everyday reading experiences and individual support needs in English. They do not repeat the three reading-band descriptions or ask learners to report script literacy. Learners can describe their abilities without technical terminology.
- Inspectable definitions live in `data/learning-profiles.json`: three reading self-reports per language, script prerequisites, version, and generation guides.
- `data/settings.json` stores enabled targets/defaults, `learnerProfiles` self-assessments, and `languageExpectations`, containing optional preferences per language, including inactive languages. There is no automatic level inference from either description.
- Every request includes the selected reading self-report, saved self-assessment, and optional language expectations alongside language, difficulty, topic and practice. Prompt instructions use these as context and reject embedded instructions. A temporary difficulty overrides the default for that story.
- The prompt adapts vocabulary, grammar and glossary support to the report. This is generator guidance; learning effects and actual difficulty remain unverified.
- Prompt version `2026-10-10.7` and profile-definition version are saved alongside the selected difficulty and the exact self-report, self-assessment, and expectation snapshot. Existing saved stories are unchanged.
